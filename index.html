EDES 120/220 · Camp For All

Parity Aim.

An adaptive stand and trigger so campers with limited hand or arm function can play paintball on their own.

Mission

Improve access to paintball for Camp For All campers with limited hand or arm function, so they can play more independently.

Mission statement

Our goal is to improve access to paintball target shooting for Camp For All campers with limited hand or arm function, allowing them to participate more independently in the activity.

The problem

At the paintball range, some campers with limited hand strength, dexterity, finger movement, or arm mobility can aim and know when they want to fire, but cannot stabilize the marker or generate the force needed to pull a traditional trigger. There is no dedicated accommodation: the range only has a table to rest the marker on. As a result, some campers need extra help or cannot take part as independently as others.

Key learnings from research

Existing solutions are too expensive

The HQ100 is the only product that meets all of the client's functional needs: chin, mouth, sip, bite or button control, recoil absorption, and a self-stabilizing base. However, it costs nearly $3,000 and has more features than the camp needs.

Current workarounds

Solenoid push-button actuators and sip-and-puff switches fire without a finger pull. Motorized pan-and-tilt mounts let players aim with a joystick or head array. Each is too costly or too narrow for every camper.

Ability varies by person

Even within one diagnosis, function varies widely. Cerebral palsy's MACS scale runs from Level I (handles objects easily) to Level V (cannot handle objects). We must design for the motion a camper can reliably make.

Low force is achievable

Commercial adaptive switches activate at about 10 g, 70 g, 100 g, and 156 g. Low-force interfaces can serve very different abilities.

Safety rules shape the design

Texas youth-camp rules treat paintball guns as pellet guns that need supervision and secure storage. ASTM F2272 requires markers not to discharge from jolts, and F1776 covers eye and face protection.

Desired features & functions

  • Detaches from the paintball marker and is modular
  • Lets campers fire with limited or no physical force, including campers with no hands or limbs
  • Stands and stabilizes on its own, holding the marker's weight
  • Lets campers aim and rotate the marker
  • Preserves the marker's existing safety features and prevents unintended firing
  • Is easy for staff to clean, adjust, and maintain
  • Contains no latex, since some campers are allergic
  • Uses Camp For All's purple and green where possible (requested as nice to have but nonessential)

Why the criteria matter

We developed seven criteria, three constraints and four objectives, from our client interview, research, and the problems campers face at the range. Safety and stability are constraints because a device that can't safely hold the paintball gun fails outright. The objectives, by contrast, allow us to measure the success of our device without deciding whether it fundamentally works.

We ranked ease of use first, because the point of the project is camper independence. A durable, cheap device that still needs constant staff help wouldn't solve the problem. Durability is second, since many campers may use the solution many times on any given day. Cost was initially placed third, though we ultimately discarded the criterion on the basis of Rice University lecturer Dr. Heather Bisesti's feedback. Maintenance is last because it affects staff more than campers, and our client explicitly stated that maintenance was not a large concern given the organization's large staff. These rankings and the design criteria they encompass guide concept selection toward the designs that most improve independence and long-term reliability.

Screening compared each idea to a datum (+ / 0 / −) on all six criteria. We screened partial ideas within each block, combined the 17 survivors in a morph chart, then screened the 30 complete ideas. Scoring rated the final 10 from 1 to 5 using measurable proxies, weighted by our objective ranking. Constraints weren't scored because every design must meet them.

Why Idea 12Ideas 12 and 14 tied at 3.4. Both rated 4/5 on ease of use and share a Y-stand mount and spring-rod trigger. We chose Idea 12 because it stays more true to the traditional paintball experience: the camper aims and fires the marker rather than steering it with head movement.

EDP 6 · Prototype concept

Staff setup

  1. Secure the elephant-method extension onto the existing trigger.
  2. Seat the marker in the slots around the pistol grip and Y-stand mount.
  3. Attach the spring + rod mechanism around the extended trigger.

Camper use

  1. Sit behind the device and place feet on the rotating foot disk.
  2. Rotate the base with the feet to aim.
  3. Fire with the elephant method or the foot button.

Manufacturing and training

The mount, elephant-method extension, and Y-stand are designed in CAD and 3D printed. The base uses OEDK bearings, with wood and metal for the stand and disks. Each part slots into the next, so integration stays simple.

Stage 04 · Early iterations

Prototype Iteration

Two iterations so far: a cardboard proof of concept, then a CAD and 3D-printed v2.

The big pivotLo-fi testing showed that Idea 12's original support and rotation mechanism didn't work as well as expected. Inspired by a pottery wheel, we redesigned it as a foot-driven rotating base that carries the marker's weight while the camper aims with their feet.

v1 · Lo-fi foot axis, Y-stand, and mount

Built from
Cardboard, straws, string, hot glue, tape, small bearings, and dowel rods, at a small scale. Each subsystem was built separately.
Purpose
Check the idea and geometry cheaply before CAD. Can the Y-stand hold the marker steady? Can a bearing turn a moving stand on top of a fixed one? Can the mount fit the marker?
Results
The Y-stand stands on its own and the axis rotates a full 360°, more than we need. The materials can't hold a real marker, but that is a limit of lo-fi materials, not of the design. The Y-stand and spring + rod trigger worked as expected.
Lessons
Add stops to limit rotation to about 120° for safety. Raise the front and back mounts above the middle to cradle the marker. The scaled-down base couldn't be connected to the full-size parts, so integration is untested.
Next
Move every component into CAD, with a larger bearing and more durable materials.

v2 · CAD mount, trigger, interface, stand, and axis

Purpose
Improve durability and precision, much closer to the device's real function.
Results
The axis turns smoothly through 360°. The stand supports the proportional weight of a 3D-printed model marker, and the mount fits snugly around it.
Feedback
Dr. Heather Bisesti suggested adding vertical adjustment to better match the paintball experience, and a push trigger so campers with limited strength can fire more easily.
Next
Build and test a push-trigger version with adjustable height, and 3D print the elephant method.
